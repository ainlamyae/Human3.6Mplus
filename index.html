Dataset & research code

Human3.6M+

Synthetic kinematic, dynamic, and muscle data for 3D human pose and motion estimation — generated from the Human3.6M dataset with a 40‑DoF musculoskeletal model.

Ali Nasr · Kevin Zhu · John McPhee — Department of Systems Design Engineering, University of Waterloo

Read the paper GitHub repository Motion Research Group Unlicense (public domain)
Abstract

Human motion capture technology is utilized in many industries, including entertainment, sports, medicine, augmented reality, virtual reality, and robotics. However, motion capture data only allows the user to analyze human movement at a kinematic level. In order to study the corresponding dynamics and muscle properties, additional sensors such as force plates and electromyography sensors are needed — collecting, processing, and synchronizing data from multiple sources can be laborious and time‑consuming.

This study proposes a method to generate the dynamics and muscle properties of existing motion capture datasets. Our method reconstructs motions via kinematics, dynamics, and muscle modeling with a musculoskeletal model consisting of 14 joints, 40 degrees of freedom, and 15 segments. Compared to current physics simulators, our method also infers muscle properties to ensure the human model is realistic, and meets International Society of Biomechanics (ISB) standards for all terminologies and representations.

The integrated musculoskeletal model allows the user to pre‑select various anthropometric features of the person performing the motion — height, mass, level of athleticism, handedness, and skin temperature — which are often infeasible to estimate from monocular video without appropriate annotations. We apply our method to the Human3.6M dataset and show that our reconstructed motion is kinematically similar to the ground‑truth markers while being dynamically plausible when compared to experimental data found in the literature. The generated data (Human3.6M+) is available for download.

Motivation

Why generate dynamics and muscle data at all?

Marker-based and marker-less motion capture both give you kinematics — where the joints are over time. They don't tell you the dynamics (joint torques, ground reaction forces) or muscle activity behind the motion, which normally requires force plates and EMG sensors collected, cleaned, and synchronized alongside the video — a slow, expensive process that has kept force-annotated motion datasets rare.

Physics-based pose estimators try to work around this by enforcing physical plausibility with general-purpose simulators and simplified rigid-body human models, but those bodies look nothing like the parametric human models (e.g. SMPL) used elsewhere in pose and shape estimation, and they can't produce muscle-level signals at all.

Human3.6M+ instead reconstructs each Human3.6M motion through a full ISB-standard musculoskeletal model — kinematics, then inverse dynamics, then a Hill-type muscle-torque-generator layer — so the same motion capture data now comes with physically-consistent joint torques and inferred muscle activation/excitation signals, ready to use as training or validation data for physics-based 3D human pose, shape, and motion estimation.

Limitations

What to keep in mind

  • Limited variability — Human3.6M covers a finite set of ~3.6M poses; it won't generalize to novel or uncommon poses on its own.
  • Limited diversity — the source dataset's subjects don't cover the full range of age, sex, body type, or clothing.
  • No ground-truth force verification — ground reaction wrenches are estimated, not measured, since Human3.6M has no force-plate data.
  • Static/temporal limits — doesn't model age-related change over time, and fast dynamic activities (sports, dance) benefit from higher-rate capture than what's available here.
  • Zero muscle co-contraction assumed — activation signals assume no agonist/antagonist co-contraction, which real sEMG data shows does occur.
